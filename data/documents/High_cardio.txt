Regular aerobic exercise (e.g. cycling, running) is beneficial for most people with heart disease or asthma, but must be tailored to individual condition and severity. Major guidelines (ESC, AHA, GINA, BTS) encourage meeting general activity targets (≥150 min/week moderate exercise) even in chronic cardiovascular disease. Exercise improves cardiovascular fitness, functional capacity and quality of life, and – with asthma – often lessens symptoms by improving lung function. However, precautions are needed: unstable cardiac conditions or uncontrolled asthma are contraindications, and intensive exercise in deconditioned patients carries a (low) risk of adverse events. Key advice is to start gradually, use thorough warm-up/cool-down, monitor intensity (via HR or RPE), optimize medications (e.g. pre-exercise bronchodilators, avoid abrupt beta-blocker effects), and watch for red-flag symptoms (chest pain, syncope, severe wheezing). In asthma, always pre-treat and avoid triggers (pollution, cold air). After any exacerbation (heart or asthma), wait until full clinical recovery before resuming, and restart at low intensity. Practical sample plans (for beginner, intermediate, advanced) are given below. All advice is drawn from current guidelines and reviews (AHA/ESC/BTS/GINA).

Safety Considerations
Pre-exercise evaluation: A physician’s clearance is wise for moderate–severe heart disease or asthma. Patients should have stable treatment (optimal meds) before intensive exercise. Follow guideline-based screening (e.g. stress test in known CAD) if high risk.
Environment/Equipment: Choose safe settings. Cycle or run on level, well-lit terrain or treadmill; avoid extremes of temperature or pollution. Monitor air quality (avoid high ozone/pollen hours). For asthma, cover the mouth/nose in cold air. Wear appropriate safety gear (helmet for cycling).
Monitoring: Continuously observe for warning signs. Use HR monitors or RPE to track intensity. For heart patients, abnormal ECG, excessive BP rise or fall are red flags. In asthma, watch for wheezing, cough or inability to speak. Pulse oximetry (keep SpO₂ ≥90%) may be helpful in severe lung/heart disease.
Termination criteria: Stop exercise immediately if there is chest pain, extreme breathlessness, dizziness, syncope, palpitations, or cyanosis. These are red flags requiring rest and possibly urgent care. For asthma, if use of inhaler fails to relieve symptoms within 10–15 min, treat as emergency.
Evidence-Based Benefits and Risks
Cardiovascular benefits: Regular aerobic training reduces mortality and morbidity in coronary artery disease (CAD) and heart failure, improves exercise capacity and functional class. Even weight-bearing (running) or non-weight-bearing (cycling) sports boost fitness and insulin sensitivity.
Asthma benefits: Regular exercise decreases airway inflammation and reduces exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) severity. Improved cardiovascular fitness aids better symptom control (less dyspnea). Guidelines stress that controlled asthma should not limit activity.
Risks in heart disease: Unaccustomed vigorous exercise transiently raises the risk of events (MI, arrhythmia), especially in low-fit individuals. However, long-term risk is lower in active people. Competitive/intense sports may provoke ischemia in severe CAD; such individuals need testing clearance.
Risks in asthma: EIB (cough, wheeze, chest tightness) can occur during/after intense exercise, especially in cold/dry air. This is managed by pre-treatment and warm-ups (below). Poorly controlled asthma raises risk of severe attack during activity. Non-respiratory risks include falls or injuries in an asthmatic patient straining to breathe.
Heart Disease – Condition-Specific Guidance
Chronic Coronary Syndrome (Stable CAD): Encourage meeting standard physical activity goals (≥150 min moderate or 75 min vigorous per week). Exercise testing is advised before high-intensity training. Stable patients should avoid competitive sports only if tests show significant ischemia or LV dysfunction. Start with walking or cycling; monitor for angina or undue fatigue. Carry nitroglycerin if prescribed.
After Acute Events: After myocardial infarction or revascularization, begin supervised cardiac rehabilitation. AHA/ACC advises initiating low-level activity early (days to weeks) under guidance, then gradual intensification. High-intensity sports only after several months of recovery and testing. Always get cardiologist clearance.
Heart Failure (HF): Exercise training is a Class I recommendation in stable HF patients. Typical prescription: aerobic (walking, cycling) 2–5 days/week, starting at moderate intensity. Warm-up 5–10 min is mandatory. Begin at 10–20 min per session and progress to 30–45 min (or longer).
Intensity: Start around 40–50% of VO₂ reserve (RPE ~11–12); progress to ~70–80% VO₂R (RPE ~13–14) continuous. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) can be safely added 1–3 days/week once moderate training is established. For example, alternate 1–4 min hard intervals (80–90% VO₂R, RPE 15–17) with recovery.
Mode: Use large muscle activities – cycling, treadmill walking/jogging, rowing, elliptical – to minimize fall risk and adjust load. HF patients often start on a cycle or treadmill for stability.
Precautions: Monitor for hypotension or arrhythmia; stop if abrupt BP drop, chest discomfort or presyncope. Avoid Valsalva (strain) during exercise. Use RPE rather than HR in patients on beta-blockers.
Arrhythmias:
Atrial Fibrillation (AF): Moderate regular exercise reduces AF risk by improving CV health. Endurance athletes (esp. men) have higher AF incidence, so vigorous training should be introduced cautiously. Patients with known AF need good rate control; exercise may be resumed if asymptomatic and ventricular rate is well controlled. In AF, stop activity if rapid palpitations or dizziness occur.
Other Supraventricular/SVT: Generally benign if no structural disease. Exercise can continue but abrupt triggers (caffeine, extreme exertion) should be avoided.
Ventricular Arrhythmias/Devices: Complex arrhythmias (e.g. VT, VF history) require cardiologist clearance. Most ICD patients can do moderate exercise, but should avoid contact sports. Post-ablation or device therapy, follow physician timing (often ~1 month convalescence).
General: Uncontrolled arrhythmias (high-grade heart block, sustained VT) are contraindications to exertion until fixed. Any new syncope/near-syncope during exercise mandates emergency evaluation.
Asthma – Condition-Specific Guidance
Exercise-Induced Bronchoconstriction (EIB): Occurs in many with asthma. Preventive steps: Always use a short-acting β₂-agonist (e.g. salbutamol) 15–30 min before exercise. Warm-up (5–10 min of gradually intensifying exercise) can itself mitigate EIB.
Mild Asthma: If well controlled on maintenance therapy, patients should be encouraged to exercise normally. They should still pre-treat before exertion to be safe. Avoid long-term over-reliance on pre-treaters by optimizing controller meds (GINA strategy: use low-dose ICS regularly).
Moderate-to-Severe Asthma: Ensure optimal controller treatment (regular inhaled corticosteroids or ICS-LABA per GINA guidelines). Even these patients can exercise if asymptomatic; usually require pre-exercise bronchodilator. More frequent or longer warm-ups (including short bouts of activity followed by brief rests) may be needed. Consider pulmonary rehab or supervised programs if deconditioned.
Triggers: Avoid exercising outdoors during high allergen/pollution days. For exercise in cold, windy conditions, wear a scarf or mask over mouth. Swimming often well tolerated (warm humid air) except chlorinated pools may irritate.
Monitoring: Watch for cough, wheeze or chest tightness during/after exercise. Use a peak flow meter if recommended (e.g. >10–15% drop post-exercise indicates significant EIB).
Contraindications: Acute exacerbation (respiratory infection or asthma flare-up) is a temporary contraindication; resume only after full recovery and bronchodilators are again effective.
Recommended Activities and Formats
Cycling vs Running: Both are effective aerobic exercises. Cycling (stationary or outdoor) is low-impact and easier on joints; it allows steady pacing and is easier for heart patients who may have orthopedic or balance limitations. Running/jogging yields higher maximal cardiorespiratory stimulus (higher VO₂), but increases joint load and potential asthma trigger (outdoor pollen). Choice depends on personal factors: e.g. if weight-bearing exercise is needed for bone health, include running/walking; if lower joint stress preferred, focus on cycling or elliptical.
Outdoor vs Indoor: Outdoor exercise provides variation and sunlight (vitamin D), but exposes one to weather/air-quality issues. Indoor (gym treadmill, stationary bike, home videos) offers environmental control. Asthmatics or those with air quality concerns may prefer indoor on high-pollen or polluted days. Both are acceptable if monitored closely.
Continuous vs Interval: Continuous moderate exercise (e.g. steady cycling or jogging for 20–60 min) is foundational. Interval training (alternating high-intensity bursts with recovery) can efficiently raise fitness – e.g. 1–4 min hard, 2–3 min easy – and is endorsed in cardiac rehab for many patients. Beginners should start with continuous. Intervals may be added later: for HF/CAD patients, intervals (up to 80–90% VO₂R) have been shown safe when supervised. In asthma, interval exercise (with pre-treatment) may actually cause less bronchospasm than constant high-intensity.
Intensity Prescription
Heart Rate (HR): For moderate intensity aim ~50–70% of age-predicted max HR (or ~40–60% heart-rate reserve). Vigorous intensity is ~70–85% HRmax. If patient is on β-blockers or has a pacemaker, HR targets will be artificially low; instead use RPE (see below) or percentage of pulse pressure.
Metabolic Equivalents (METs): Rough guide: moderate intensity = 3–6 METs (e.g. brisk walking or easy cycling) and vigorous >6 METs (e.g. jogging, cycling uphill). Note MET tables exist but monitor symptom response.
Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE): Use Borg 6–20 scale if HR unreliable. RPE 11–12 (“light”) corresponds to moderate exercise; RPE 15–17 is vigorous. Encourage a talk test: moderate = can speak a sentence; vigorous = can only say a few words between breaths.
Adjustments: Always start sessions at “light” intensity and ensure patient recovers well. Increase intensity by ~5% or one RPE unit every 1–2 weeks if no issues. In heart failure, some programs recommend not increasing beyond moderate-vigorous (RPE ≤14) until supervised stress testing is done.
Duration and Frequency
Aim for at least 30 minutes per session, most days (total ≥150 min/week). However, initial prescriptions may be shorter: e.g. 10–15 min per session, 2–3 days/week, then gradually ramp up. The HF guidelines suggest starting with 15–30 min sessions and progressing to 45–60 min as tolerated.
Frequency: Begin with 2–3 days/week, increasing to 3–5 days as fitness allows. Some interval HIIT protocols use 1–3 days/week of interval sessions (alternating with continuous days).
Progression: Follow the FITT principle. E.g. add 5 min to session length per week, or raise intensity slightly only if no adverse symptoms. Always alternate hard sessions with easier or rest days.
Warm-up and Cool-down
Warm-Up (5–10 min): Start every session with very light activity (slow walk or easy pedal) and dynamic stretches. Gradually increase heart rate and circulation. This prepares the heart and lungs and can markedly reduce exercise-induced asthma symptoms.
Cool-Down (5–10 min): End each session by tapering back to low intensity, followed by gentle static stretches of major muscle groups. This helps prevent blood pooling and arrhythmias. Cool-down also helps flush out exercise metabolites and reduce delayed-onset muscle soreness.
Breathing Techniques
Diaphragmatic breathing: Encourage slow, deep inhalations (belly breathing) rather than shallow chest breathing, to maximize ventilation. This is especially helpful during running.
Rhythmic breathing: Sync breathing with steps (e.g. inhale every two steps, exhale two steps) when running to avoid breath-holding.
Pursed-lip breathing: If dyspneic during exertion (often in asthma or heart failure), exhale through pursed lips to slow expiration and improve gas exchange.
Avoid Valsalva: Instruct patients not to hold breath during exertion or isometric effort; this can spike blood pressure dangerously.
Medication Timing
Asthma Medications: Take short-acting bronchodilator ~15–30 min before exercise. If on as-needed ICS-formoterol (GINA step approach), use it before exercise as reliever. Long-acting controllers (ICS or LABA) should be maintained daily. Ensure rescue inhaler is accessible at all times during exercise.
Heart Medications: Continue baseline meds (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, diuretics, etc.). Beta-blockers will blunt peak HR – in these patients, rely more on RPE. If prescribed, sublingual nitroglycerin can be used prophylactically before exertion prone to angina (with physician guidance) and should be carried during exercise.
Hydration and Timing: Avoid exercising on a full stomach or if dehydrated. Patients on diuretics should time exercise away from peak diuresis to prevent dehydration.
Monitoring During Exercise
Symptoms to monitor: Chest pain/pressure, undue fatigue, lightheadedness, dizziness, palpitations or excessive dyspnea. In asthma, watch for cough, wheeze, or a prolonged recovery.
Signs: Check pulse regularly; note any irregularities. If available, periodically measure blood pressure (post-activity) and SpO₂. Significant declines in O₂ saturation (>4% drop from baseline or <90%) warrant caution.
Perceived effort: Ask patient to self-rate exertion and breathing. An RPE above 17 or feeling “too hard” means slow down.
Recording: Keep an exercise diary (duration, intensity, symptoms). This helps detect patterns (e.g. asthma triggered by certain environments). Some patients may track peak flow or HR.
Contraindications and Red Flags
Absolute (do NOT exercise): Unstable angina, acute myocardial infarction, decompensated heart failure (resting dyspnea, edema), uncontrolled arrhythmias, severe aortic stenosis, acute myocarditis/pericarditis. Recent surgery (e.g. open-heart, lung) until cleared. Severe pulmonary hypertension.
Relative/temporary: Uncontrolled hypertension (>180/110), febrile illness, unstable diabetes (hypo/hyperglycemia risk), recent pulmonary embolism or DVT until treated, uncorrected severe electrolyte imbalance.
Asthma-specific: Ongoing respiratory infection, severe exacerbation requiring hospitalization or continuous steroids, persistent O₂ desaturation.
Red Flags During Exercise: Chest discomfort, extreme breathlessness (beyond normal training effect), irregular heartbeats, light-headedness, fainting, cyanosis of lips. Stop immediately and seek medical help if these occur.
Emergency Actions
Chest pain (possible angina/MI): Stop activity. Sit or lie down. Take sublingual nitroglycerin if prescribed. If pain persists >5 min or is new/worsening, call emergency services.
Syncope or severe hypotension: Lay the person flat with legs elevated, check vital signs, and call ambulance.
Severe asthma attack: Stop exercise, sit upright, use rescue inhaler (administer 2–4 puffs SABA). If no improvement in 15 min or if fatigue/altered consciousness, seek emergency care.
Arrhythmia/chest discomfort: Have AED available in group settings. If collapse with no pulse, begin CPR.
Bleeding/injury: Apply first aid (pressure, immobilization) and seek help as needed.
Return-to-Exercise Criteria After Exacerbation or Event
Cardiac Event (MI, PCI, HF exacerbation): Wait until medically stabilized. Typically, light activity (walking) can start in days; more vigorous exercise only after several weeks and physician clearance. A graded cardiac rehab program is recommended, with staged increase of intensity once LV function and symptoms have improved.
Arrhythmia Event: After cardioversion/ablation for AF, wait ~4 weeks (with resting rhythm normalized) before resuming intense training. Patients should be on anticoagulation (if indicated) before returning to contact sports.
Asthma Exacerbation: Resume exercise only when symptoms are gone and lung function is near baseline (e.g. peak flow >80%). Start at a lower intensity and use bronchodilator prophylaxis.
Practical Modifications & Training Plans
General Principles: Progress slowly, tailoring to tolerance. Sessions should always include a 5–10 min warm-up and cool-down. Below are illustrative plans for an average adult (modify for age, comorbidities, medications). Emphasize safety and listening to the body.

Beginner Plan (low fitness/first return): 2–3 days/week of aerobic activity. Start with 10–15 minutes of continuous exercise at light intensity (RPE 11–12). For example:

Warm-up 5 min (easy walk/cycle), then 10 min brisk walking or gentle cycling, then 5 min cool-down.
Add 2–5 minutes to the main exercise each week (e.g. 15→20→25 min).
If continuous 15 min is too hard, break into intervals: e.g. 5 min on/3 min off, repeat twice, then cool-down.
After 4–6 weeks, aim for 20–30 min continuous, then gradually increase frequency to 3–5 days.
Moderate Plan (improving fitness): 3–5 days/week. 30–45 min per session, combining modes. Example week:

2 continuous sessions: 30–40 min at moderate intensity (e.g. brisk walking, steady cycling).
1–2 interval sessions: e.g. 5 min warm-up, then 4× (3 min vigorous effort, 2 min easy), then 5 min cool-down. During vigorous periods aim for RPE 13–15.
1 cross-training day: e.g. 20–30 min swimming or group aerobics.
Ensure at least 1 full rest or flexibility day.
Advanced Plan (well-conditioned): 4–6 days/week. 45–60 min sessions. Include variety: running, cycling, rowing, etc. Example:

2 high-intensity sessions: E.g. 8×2 min at high effort (RPE 15–17) with 2 min recovery, total session ~45 min.
2 moderate sessions: 45–60 min at RPE 12–13 (steady-state).
1 long-endurance day: 60 min continuous at moderate pace.
Optional strength: 1–2 days of light resistance training (bodyweight, bands) to complement aerobic work, 20–30 min (see HF table for guidance).
Session Templates: All templates include warm-up/cool-down. Example for a single session:

Warm-up (5–10 min): Slow walking or easy pedaling + dynamic stretches (leg swings, arm circles).
Main Set (15–45 min): Depending on plan: steady-state or intervals as above. Keep exertion in target zone (monitor HR/RPE).
Cool-down (5–10 min): Gradually slow pace, then stretch major muscles (quads, hamstrings, calves, chest, shoulders).
Asthma-Specific: Begin every session with breathing drills (in-out nasal or deep diaphragmatic breaths). During exercise, patients may choose nasal breathing when possible. If exercise triggers mild symptoms, switch to a lighter pace or take a brief rest. Always carry rescue inhaler and re-dose if exercise will exceed 30–45 min (for prolonged activity, a second 2–4 puff dose may be needed after a couple hours).

Note: These plans assume an adult with stable, moderate heart disease or asthma. For older patients or those with severe limitations, starting intervals of 30–60 s with equal rest may be appropriate, building up as tolerated. Conversely, young/athletic patients might exceed these outlines under supervision. Personalize all plans, and stop or modify at the first sign of undue symptoms.

Sources: Recommendations above are based on current guidelines (ESC 2020 sports cardiology, AHA/ACC heart failure guidelines, AHA physical activity recommendations, British Thoracic/GINA asthma strategy) and recent reviews. When specific guidance was unavailable, expert consensus was inferred.
