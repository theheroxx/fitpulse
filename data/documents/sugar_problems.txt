1. Exercise can improve blood-glucose control and overall cardiovascular health.
2. The best exercise depends on glucose level, diabetes status, medication, fitness, and complications.
3. For people with normal or near-normal glucose, regular moderate activity is generally appropriate.
4. Brisk walking is one of the safest and most accessible starting exercises.
5. Cycling, swimming, dancing, and similar aerobic activities are also good options.
6. A general target for adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week.
7. This can be divided across at least three days rather than performed all at once.
8. Avoid going more than two consecutive days without physical activity when possible.
9. Strength training should generally be included about two to three times per week.
10. Suitable strength exercises include resistance bands, weights, wall push-ups, and chair exercises.
11. People with prediabetes can particularly benefit from regular aerobic and resistance exercise.
12. Walking after meals can be a practical way to increase daily activity and support glucose control.
13. Short activity sessions can be accumulated throughout the day.
14. Someone who is currently inactive should begin with short, comfortable sessions.
15. Starting with 5–10 minutes of walking can be appropriate for previously inactive people.
16. Exercise duration and intensity can then be increased gradually.
17. Moderate intensity generally means breathing faster while still being able to talk.
18. For mildly elevated blood glucose, moderate aerobic exercise is often a useful choice.
19. Brisk walking is usually preferable to immediately starting very intense exercise.
20. Cycling at a moderate pace can also be appropriate for many people with elevated glucose.
21. Swimming provides aerobic exercise with relatively low joint impact.
22. Resistance training can improve muscular glucose utilization and physical fitness.
23. Combining aerobic and resistance exercise is generally more effective than relying on only one type.
24. For persistently high glucose, exercise should not automatically become more intense.
25. Very high glucose requires additional caution, particularly in people with type 1 diabetes.
26. If glucose is very high and ketones are present, strenuous exercise should be avoided.
27. People with very high glucose should follow their diabetes care team's sick-day or ketone guidance.
28. Exercise can sometimes increase glucose temporarily during intense activity.
29. Therefore, glucose responses to exercise can differ substantially between individuals.
30. People using insulin have a greater risk of exercise-related hypoglycemia.
31. Some insulin-stimulating medicines can also increase hypoglycemia risk during exercise.
32. Blood glucose may fall during exercise and remain lower for hours afterward.
33. People at risk of hypoglycemia may need glucose checks before, during, and after exercise.
34. A person with low blood glucose should treat the low before exercising.
35. For many people with diabetes, glucose below 70 mg/dL is considered hypoglycemia.
36. The exact safe threshold can differ according to the person's medical plan.
37. Fast-acting carbohydrate should be available when hypoglycemia is a possibility.
38. The commonly recommended treatment for significant hypoglycemia is 15–20 grams of carbohydrate.
39. Glucose should be rechecked after treatment according to the individual's diabetes plan.
40. Exercise should generally be paused until the low glucose episode has been corrected.
41. People with frequent exercise-related lows should discuss medication and nutrition adjustments with their clinician.
42. Long or strenuous workouts can increase the likelihood of delayed hypoglycemia.
43. Resistance training may be useful alongside aerobic exercise for people with diabetes.
44. Beginners should prioritize technique, manageable resistance, and gradual progression.
45. Balance exercises can be useful, particularly for older adults.
46. Stretching can complement aerobic and resistance training but should not replace them.
47. People with diabetic neuropathy need special attention to foot protection and exercise selection.
48. People with diabetic eye complications may need to avoid certain high-strain activities.
49. People with cardiovascular, kidney, or other complications may require individualized exercise guidance.
50. Low-impact activities can be preferable when joint, foot, or mobility problems exist.
51. Sedentary time should also be interrupted with brief periods of movement when possible.
52. Standing, walking, or light activity breaks can complement structured exercise.
53. Exercise should be enjoyable enough to maintain consistently over the long term.
54. Hydration is important, especially during prolonged or warm-weather exercise.
55. Water is generally sufficient for most moderate exercise sessions.
56. People should monitor how different exercise types affect their individual glucose response.
57. A glucose meter or continuous glucose monitor can help identify exercise-related patterns.
58. Medication doses should not be changed solely on the basis of general exercise advice.
59. People with diabetes should discuss unusual glucose responses, recurrent lows, or very high readings with their healthcare team.
60. Overall, consistent moderate aerobic activity plus resistance training is the strongest general exercise foundation for blood-glucose management.
